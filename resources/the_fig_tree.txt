I was a very outgoing kid in middle school. The teachers’ favorite, the leader of every project, the top student in exams… It all started when I was sent to a high school I didn’t want to attend. Social anxiety. By the end of the year of online education in high school, I couldn’t even ride the bus alone; I couldn’t control my panic attacks. Then a boy came into my life. He took hours-long bus rides with me, forced me to talk while we shopped, and believed I could do it. After nearly two years of effort, all that remained was my stress disorder. But the pressure of my exam year—preparing for a language exam at a science-based school, studying for hours every day, and the monotony of staying in the school dorm just to avoid wasting time for the exam—drained me. I blamed all that exhaustion on the person who had been in my life and broke up with him. Perhaps that is the only regret of my life. 
There is a lesson to be learned from every mistake. The path that brought you to where you are today is paved with your wrong decisions. But some mistakes are simply mistakes. 
My first love was the most productive period of my life, in terms of art and literature. After all, your first love is the time when it’s easiest to reach the peak of emotions needed to create art. My greatest fear back then was never reading the book that would become my favorite or never encountering the film that would change my life. 
“I saw my life branching out before me like the green fig tree in the story. From the tip of every branch, like a fat purple fig, a wonderful future beckoned and winked. One fig was a husband and a happy home and children, and another fig was a famous poet and another fig was a brilliant professor, and another fig was Ee Gee, the amazing editor, and another fig was Europe and Africa and South America, and another fig was Constantin and Socrates and Attila and a pack of other lovers with queer names and offbeat professions, and another fig was an Olympic lady crew champion, and beyond and above these figs were many more figs I couldn't quite make out. I saw myself sitting in the crotch of this fig tree, starving to death, just because I couldn't make up my mind which of the figs I would choose. I wanted each and every one of them, but choosing one meant losing all the rest, and, as I sat there, unable to decide, the figs began to wrinkle and go black, and, one by one, they plopped to the ground at my feet.” (Sylvia Plath, The Bell Jar)
I actually wanted to be a psychiatrist, but I couldn’t bring myself to study medicine. I wanted to live in Kadıköy. During the times I couldn’t bring myself to switch fields, I considered studying architecture, planning to do a minor degree in literature to achieve both material and spiritual fulfillment. I wanted to live in Samsun. Then I realized I was just settling for the most tolerable option among the math-science departments, just to meet expectations and not waste the years of effort I’d already put in. I wanted to live in Edinburgh. My biggest act of defiance was writing only “literature” on my preference list.
Changing cities for university. The struggle of a girl not allowed to leave her neighborhood, moving to another city for a major that wasn’t supported, trying to stand on her own two feet. The hardest part is not being able to complain when you’re stuck—after all, you chose this life. You couldn’t properly express the pain of a breakup because you were the one who chose to leave. The circumstances under which you made that decision didn’t matter much; after all, those words came out of your own mouth. You chose literature because it was your only option for living a mentally healthy university life. So, do you remember how you never thought you’d make it from 19 to 20? Your life plans ended after you graduated from high school. You were so convinced you’d commit suicide at 19 that you entered your 20th birthday in tears. You cried because you’d succeeded, but there were still some things you couldn’t get past. You’re 20 now, and every time you reach a point where you feel the need to harm yourself, you get a tattoo.
You have more friends here than ever before. You’re surrounded by people who always make you feel loved and who value you deeply. But what if you hadn’t broken up with that guy, what if you’d chosen to stay in your family’s city, what if you’d studied medicine as your family always wanted...? Have you been able to read all the books you wanted? No, you haven’t been able to read books unless you had to since your second year of high school. Have you managed to overcome your anxiety? You still have panic attacks and faint at the slightest situation that is out of your control. Has someone like the person in your dreams entered your life? You know, we’re even starting to doubt if such a person even exists. It’s a good thing you never planned anything after age 19; I’m sure it wouldn’t have turned out the way you planned. Are we on the right path, then? Actually, we’ll never be able to know.
Whether you regret bringing them into your life or not, we’re actually made up of pieces of every person we’ve met and spoken to. Have you noticed that day by day, you’re starting to resemble that 17-year-old boy you broke up with back in high school, believing you had no common interests? Now you watch the sports he watched; you speak like him and you dress in his style. If you were going to end up this similar, why did you part ways? It had to be that way back then. You’re starting to resemble your mother more and more; your language of love is the same as your father’s. You even share common personality traits with your older brother, the one you used to fear resembling so much. 
You try to hold people’s hands because you were always taught to be strong enough to deal with things on your own. Now you can cry in front of people, for example. You still haven’t figured out your anger issues; when you get angry, you just retreat into your shell, so you don’t hurt the ones you love with your words. You know you can regret your own choices—you’ve let go of that “I never regret any of my decisions” attitude. You’re growing every day, and the number of people who’ve witnessed the whole story is dwindling. The day will come when even you’ll forget the puzzle pieces that make up your character. But that’s okay, you can always step outside the frame and create different outcomes with the same pieces. 
Do you remember wanting to study fine arts in your last year of middle school? Good thing you didn’t, you enjoy being an art historian more than being an artist. If painting had been a necessity for you, you would’ve lost interest by your first year and lost your most important escape way. If you hadn’t gotten your first tattoo in secret from your family in high school, they’d still think you’d live your life according to their wishes. It’s a good thing you didn’t major in English literature—your favorite classes aren’t the literature courses as you expected. You actually enjoy learning about American politics and history much more. If you’d stayed in Istanbul, for instance, you’d never have grown up in your family’s eyes.
You still can’t decide whether you want to be an editor, a scriptwriter or an academician. Will you stay in Ankara or return to Istanbul? What about Edinburgh, can you be able to adapt to a whole different culture rather than your own nation’s? Do you want a dog or a cat? What about having children? Maybe one day you’ll open your own art gallery, maybe you won’t have a slightest chance to continue painting. This fig or that fig?
If only, fortunately, if only, fortunately, if only, fortunately, maybe…
If you think about it, they don’t really mean much. Life moves forward as it’s supposed to; time doesn’t stop waiting for you to make a decision. The way to come out of this process with the least damage is to listen to your heart. Set aside the “maybe” and “if only” and focus on the positive outcomes. Believe me, it’s unbearable otherwise. And get a tattoo once in a while—it really clears things up.
